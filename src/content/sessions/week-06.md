Your work goes up on a wall at ten past the hour, and you are not allowed to
stand next to it.

That is the entire mechanism. An artefact you can explain is not the same
artefact as one that has to survive a stranger reading it at speed with you out
of the room, and the second is the only one you ever actually submit.

## Before you arrive

Print or pin one artefact, in draft, fitted to one named target from Dossier §2.
Draft is the correct state — a finished piece attracts compliments and a draft
attracts information. Bring three copies of nothing; the sheet beside your work
is where everything is written.

## The three walls

The room is organised by surface rather than by stream, in the three columns the
[lecture deck](/decks/week-06-varnish/) uses:

- **Technical Practice** starts at the repository wall: front page and README,
  read by someone deciding in ninety seconds whether to clone it.
- **Creative and Communications Practice** starts at the portfolio wall:
  sequencing and case-study structure, read by someone who will stop at a
  specific slide and can tell you which one.
- **Commercial and Public Practice** starts at the brief wall: a one-page
  capability brief, read by someone who has thirty of them.

## How the laps run

Four laps, and the order is not negotiable.

1. **Silent lap.** You read, you write on the sheet, you do not speak. Your
   critique names a decision, not a finish: "the second paragraph claims scale
   with no number" rather than "nice layout".
2. **Second lap, your own wall.** You read your own sheet in silence. You may
   not reply to it and you may not explain.
3. **Response lap.** You write, on your own sheet, which critiques you accept
   and which you reject, each with a reason. Rejecting one with a reason is a
   perfectly good answer and is scored the same.
4. **Cross-stream lap.** The last twenty minutes, and the point of the week:
   each stream walks the other two walls.

## Why the cross-stream lap is the point

Your stream's conventions feel like quality until you watch another field break
them without penalty. Engineers discover a README's plainness is a house style.
Designers discover the sequencing instinct they thought was universal is a
sector norm. The commercial room discovers its one-pager reads as evasive to
everyone else. What you take away is not another field's conventions — it is the
knowledge that yours are conventions, which is the only way to vary them on
purpose.

Critique is written, always. It becomes your evidence for
[Assessment 3](/assessments/03-peer-critique/), and a compliment given aloud
leaves no receipt for anybody.

## Leaving with

Dossier §7: the target-fitted artefact in draft, plus the first entries in your
**Varnish Note** — every presentational decision you made, and what each one
reveals or conceals. Thin and thick both get recorded. The Note is a quarter of
[Assessment 2](/assessments/02-performance-materials/) and it is the entry
markers read first.

Then the teaching break, then [week 7](/sessions/week-07/). Build during the
break. Do not polish.
