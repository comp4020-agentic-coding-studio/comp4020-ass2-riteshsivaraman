Sunil Raman starts a visible ninety-second timer, opens a volunteered
application, and narrates what he is doing to it while the clock runs. He has
done this professionally for eleven years, across roughly the number of
applications that makes people wince when he says it out loud.

## Ninety seconds is not an insult

It is arithmetic. Two hundred applications and a shortlisting meeting on Friday
produce ninety seconds per document whether anybody approves or not. The
document is not being read; it is being verified. Those are different
activities and only one of them rewards nice sentences.

What happens in the ninety seconds: a claim is spotted, an eye moves looking
for the thing that backs it, and either finds it or does not. Nobody is
searching for reasons to reject you — they are searching for something they can
check, because a checkable candidate is a safe recommendation to make to their
own manager.

## Receipt debt

Your receipt debt is the accumulated distance between what your materials claim
and what you could evidence inside that window. It is invisible while nobody
asks. It is charged in full, at once, the first time somebody says show me,
which is usually in front of three people.

Debt is not fraud. Most of it is true work with no artefact attached: the thing
you built and never wrote up, the project you ran and never recorded a number
for. The lecture's argument is that the write-up is not overhead on the work —
for a stranger, it is the work, because it is all of it that travels.

## Converting activity into artefacts

Four conversions, done on screen: a project becomes a front page with a
problem, a decision and an outcome; a task becomes a number with a before and
an after; a semester of study becomes one thing that runs; a group effort
becomes a named part with the constraint attached.

Each takes under an hour. That ratio — an hour of writing against a semester of
unverifiable work — is the whole lecture, and it is why week 4 sits here rather
than at the end.
