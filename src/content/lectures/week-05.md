Students arrive at this course believing they need a story for every question.
They need three, told well enough that each one holds up under four different
questions without buckling. That is what makes an anecdote load-bearing: not
that it is impressive, but that it takes weight from several directions.

## Preparation is not dishonesty

The objection comes up every year, so it is dealt with first. Rehearsing a true
account of something you did is not fabrication; it is the difference between
your listener hearing what happened and your listener hearing you reconstruct it
in real time under pressure. Improvising badly is not more authentic. It is just
less legible, and legibility is the whole channel.

The line is unchanged from week 1. Framing what is true is practice. Claiming
what is not is fraud. A rehearsed story crosses that line at exactly one point:
when the rehearsal starts improving the facts.

## The structure

Four parts, in this order, and the order is the point.

**Situation** — enough context to make the decision make sense, and not one
sentence more. **Decision** — what you chose, and what you chose against.
**Consequence** — what followed, including when what followed was mediocre.
**What you would change** — which is the part that signals you have thought about
it since.

Most students over-invest in situation and skip decision entirely, which
produces a well-told story that scores nothing.

## Two failure modes

The **decisionless story**: things happened, you were nearby, the outcome was
good. A panel cannot distinguish that from luck, so it scores it as luck.

The **borrowed outcome**: the team shipped, the campaign performed, the
submission succeeded. Told in the first person plural it sounds generous and
lands as evasive. Say your part first, in the active voice, then the team's.
This is the same integrity boundary week 6 raises about the word "we", and the
easiest of all overclaims to detect.
