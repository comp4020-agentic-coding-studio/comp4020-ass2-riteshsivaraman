Three practitioners, one from each field, fifteen minutes each, in the one
shared slot. You hear all three, including the two from fields you are not in,
and that is the point rather than an accident of timetabling.

Each guest is hosted by their stream's convenor and is given the same brief: not
career advice in general, but the specific question they wish entry-level
candidates had asked them, and what not asking it cost those candidates. They
are asked to be concrete and to name figures where their field allows it.

- **Technical** — hosted by Anselm Pike. What scope and level actually mean on
  an offer, what a team allocation commits you to for a year, and how much
  unpaid work a take-home is quietly assuming.
- **Creative and Communications** — hosted by Nadia Ferrante. Rates, usage
  rights, and the test brief. The rights conversation is the one nobody has at
  twenty-two and everybody re-litigates at twenty-eight.
- **Commercial and Public** — hosted by Sunil Raman. What is genuinely fixed by
  policy, what is not, and why the graduate who asks about rotation preference
  in the right week gets it while the one who asks in the wrong week does not.

Hearing the other two fields does something the negotiation reading cannot. You
discover that your own conventions are conventions: the technical students learn
that an unpaid test brief is a live ethical argument somewhere, and the creative
students learn that a salary band can be a published document. Both leave with a
better sense of what the negotiation flinch is actually defending against.

Guests appear as roles rather than as personalities and are not recruiting. Take
notes on questions, not on people — the questions transfer between organisations
and the people do not.

Fifteen minutes each leaves a quarter of an hour at the end for cross-field
questions, which is where the useful disagreements happen. Bring one.
[This afternoon's tutorial](/sessions/week-10/) is where you say the number out
loud, so arrive at it with the number already chosen.
