§01

Shared lecture — the pre-warmed stranger

Cold outreach has a bad reputation because most of it is a stranger asking another stranger for free labour while pretending otherwise. The lecture teaches the alternative: a message that pre-warms itself, by being specific enough about the recipient’s actual work that answering it costs them less than ignoring it would.

The honesty constraint is stated first and does not move: never misrepresent who you are or why you are writing. You are a student, you are asking because you want to learn something specific, and the message says so in the first line. Every rewrite this week is a rewrite for clarity and specificity, never a rewrite that edges toward implying a relationship or a status you do not have.

The second half is about the sending decision, because it is a genuine decision and not a formality. Sending is optional, never required: conscripting a non-consenting stranger into an assessment runs against the same principle that governs the week-9 practice interview pool. The Outreach Log records what you drafted, what you sent or chose not to, and why, and both routes are marked the same way.

Silence is taught as the standard reply, not a verdict. Most well-drafted, well-targeted messages get no reply at all, for reasons that have nothing to do with the message’s quality. Treating silence as information about yourself is the most expensive mistake this week can teach, and the lecture spends real time on why the instinct is so strong anyway.

Bring a name: one specific person in your field whose actual work you could write three sentences about without checking notes. You will draft to them today, whether or not you decide to send it.

What this lecture covers

  1. Cold open: three real messages, one that got a reply and two that did not. You guess which.
  2. The honesty constraint, stated and held to for the rest of the semester.
  3. Answerability in one line: what makes a message cheap to reply to.
  4. Sending as a choice, and why the Outreach Log grades both routes identically.
  5. Silence as the standard reply, not a verdict.
  6. Handover to the outreach sprint in stream sessions.
§02

Stream sessions — the outreach sprint, three angles

A

Technical Practice — maintainers and meetup organisers

75 min · seminar room 2 · bring three targets

Targets this week are open-source maintainers, engineering-blog authors, and meetup organisers — people whose public work you can name specifically. A message that says which commit or which post prompted you costs them nothing to answer.

The failure mode this stream sees most is asking for a job in the first message. This week is not that. It is asking one specific, answerable question about their work, drafted well enough to send even if you decide not to.

In session

Draft three messages in the room. Peers score each for answerability in one line: could the recipient reply usefully in under thirty seconds?

B

Creative and Communications Practice — named practitioners

75 min · studio floor · bring three targets

Targets are practitioners whose specific piece you can speak about — not a vague admiration of their “work” in general, but one piece, one decision in it, one question you actually have. Generic flattery is the fastest way to signal a template.

This stream drafts toward people who might one day critique or commission work, so the honesty constraint matters doubly: you are a student asking a genuine question, not a peer pretending equal footing.

In session

Draft three messages, each naming one specific piece. Peers score each for answerability; the group flags any message that reads as a pitch rather than a question.

C

Commercial and Public Practice — alumni and liaisons

75 min · workshop · bring three targets

Targets are alumni in the target field, industry-body contacts, and graduate-program liaisons — people whose role is partly to be reachable. That makes the specificity bar higher, not lower: a generic alumni message is the one this stream drafts most and sends least.

The useful question is usually about the path they took, not about an opening. Asking about a role directly reads as an application in disguise, and the honesty constraint forbids exactly that disguise.

In session

Draft three messages targeting the path, not the vacancy. Peers score each for answerability and flag anything that smuggles in a job ask.

    Course reader, chapter seven. Never misrepresent who you are or why you are writing, and what that rules out in practice.

    Required · 18 pp

    A short essay on why silence is the standard reply and what it does and does not tell you about the message.

    Optional · 5 pp
§04

The activity

Draft the Outreach Log

Draft three messages to three specific people. Deciding not to send any of them is a legitimate outcome — the log records the decision either way, with the reasoning behind it.

Time
75 minutes, in your stream session
Needs
Three named people, one specific thing each
Produces
Three drafts, a send decision for each
Collected
No. Feeds the Outreach Log in your Dossier

  1. Name three people whose specific work you can speak about without checking notes.

  2. Draft one message to each, naming the specific thing and asking one answerable question.

  3. Trade drafts with a peer. They score each for answerability in one line.

  4. Decide, for each draft, whether to send it. Neither choice needs defending to the group.

  5. Record the decision and the reason in the Outreach Log, whether or not you sent it.
