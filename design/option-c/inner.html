Registration and the off-register

What happens when two layers of the same work are laid down slightly out of line — and why the gap between them is usually the most informative thing on the page.

01 · Shared

The lecture

Registration is the problem of getting one layer to land exactly on top of another. In two-colour printing it is a mechanical question: the sheet moves through the press twice, and it never goes through twice the same way. A fraction of a millimetre of drift and the second ink sits beside the first instead of on it.

Where the useful information sits

Printers spend a great deal of effort eliminating that drift, which is reasonable — but it means the drift itself is rarely examined. This week we take the opposite position. An out-of-register print is a record of the machine’s behaviour, written in the only language the machine has. The misalignment tells you about pressure, about paper stretch, about which direction the grain ran. None of that is visible in a print that came out correctly.

The claim generalises past printing, and that is why it is a shared lecture rather than a stream session. Any process that runs twice over the same material leaves a registration trace: a second draft over a first, a repeated experiment, a rehearsal against a performance, a design intent against what was actually built. In each case the interesting measurement is not either layer on its own. It is the offset.

The gap between what you meant to do and what the material let you do is not an error term. It is the finding.

Lecture 07 · opening claim

Three ways the offset is read

We will work through three readings of the same off-register sheet, because they conflict and the conflict is instructive. The first treats the offset as noise to be characterised and subtracted. The second treats it as a signature — a way of identifying which press, which hand, which session produced a given artefact. The third treats it as an aesthetic resource, something to be induced deliberately at a magnitude you choose.

All three are defensible. What is not defensible is using one of them without noticing that you have chosen. By the end of the session you should be able to say which reading you are operating under in your own stream work this week, and what the other two would have had you do instead.

What to bring

Two versions of something you have already made this semester, where the second was meant to be a corrected version of the first. Any medium. We overlay them in the second half of the session and you annotate your own offsets in front of the room.

Fabric & Structure

Stream A · studio · 3 hrs · bench 4–11

Induce the offset deliberately

You will build the same small structure twice from identical stock, using the same drawings, and then measure every way the second one differs from the first. The point is not to get better at the second attempt. It is to produce a defensible account of where a repeated build drifts and why.

Work in pairs but build separately — you need two independent second attempts to tell a systematic drift from a personal habit. If both of you drift the same direction, the cause is in the material or the jig. If you drift differently, the cause is in your hands, and that is a different kind of finding with a different remedy.

Bring both objects to Friday’s cross-stream review intact. Do not repair the worse one. A repaired object cannot be measured.

  1. Cut stock for two identical builds. Label every piece before you start; unlabelled offcuts are the most common way this session fails.
  2. Build the first without timing yourself. Record the finished dimensions at nine fixed points.
  3. Build the second from the same drawings. Same nine measurements, same order, same instrument.
  4. Tabulate the nine offsets. Sign and date the table before you look at your partner’s.
  5. Write two hundred words naming which reading of the offset you are using, and what the other two would have told you to do.
Session shape 0:00 Bench setup, stock check 0:15 Build one, untimed 1:20 Measure, nine points 1:40 Build two 2:35 Measure, tabulate 2:50 Sign and file

The nine points have to be chosen before the first build. Choosing them after is how you accidentally prove whatever you already believed.

Take to Friday: two objects, one signed table, two hundred words. Nothing else is collected.

Blocking & Facilitation

Stream B · seminar · 2 hrs · paired rotation

Run it twice, change one variable

Your registration problem is a session run twice with the same run sheet and a different room. You will facilitate a fifteen-minute discussion on this week’s lecture claim, then hand your run sheet to your partner and watch them facilitate the identical fifteen minutes with a different group.

What you are measuring is the offset between the written structure and what the room actually did with it — twice, by two different people. A run sheet that produces the same shape in both hands is robust. One that only works when its author runs it is a performance, not a structure, and you should say so in your notes rather than defending it.

The hard discipline here is not interrupting. While your partner runs your sheet you take timestamped notes and say nothing, including when they are clearly about to lose the group. The recovery is data.

  1. Write a fifteen-minute run sheet: an opening question, two turns, a close. One page, timestamped, no notes to self.
  2. Run it. Your partner observes silently and timestamps.
  3. Swap. Run their sheet with a different group while they observe yours.
  4. Lay the two observation sets side by side and mark every point where the two runs diverged by more than ninety seconds.
  5. Annotate your own sheet in ink where the structure, not the facilitator, caused the divergence.
Session shape 0:00 Run sheets written 0:25 Run one, observed 0:45 Reset, swap groups 0:55 Run two, observed 1:20 Side-by-side marking 1:45 Annotate and file

Silence while your sheet fails is the whole assessment. Write down the moment you wanted to step in.

Take to Friday: one annotated run sheet, two timestamped observation sets, divergence marks in ink.

Archive & Evidence

Stream C · desk work · 2 hrs · archive access

Reconstruct, then compare

You will take a week of somebody else’s archive from earlier in the semester and reconstruct what they did from the record alone — no conversation, no questions. Then you will show them your reconstruction and record, precisely, where it departed from what actually happened.

That departure is the registration offset of an archive: the distance between the events and the retrievable account of them. Every archive has one. An archive that claims not to is either very small or lying, and this session is mostly about learning to estimate the size of yours.

You are not grading their archive and they are not grading yours. You are both producing a measurement, and the measurement is only useful if neither of you has an interest in it coming out small.

  1. Draw a week at random. Read the whole record before writing anything down.
  2. Reconstruct the week as a dated narrative with a confidence mark against each claim.
  3. Hand it over. Let them mark it up without discussion.
  4. Classify each departure: absent from the record, present but ambiguous, or present and misread by you.
  5. Write one change to your own archive practice that the third category — not the first — makes necessary.
Session shape 0:00 Draw a week, read fully 0:30 Reconstruct with confidence marks 1:05 Exchange and mark up 1:30 Classify departures 1:50 One practice change, written

Most people fix the gaps and ignore the misreadings. The misreadings are the ones your own archive will do to you.

Take to Friday: the reconstruction, the marked-up copy, a classified departure list, one practice change.

04 · In the room

The activity

Overlay, in front of the room

Twenty minutes, whole cohort, no slides. Bring your two versions. You will put the second on top of the first on the overhead, mark the offset in orange at three points you choose, and say one sentence about which of the three readings you are using.

Then somebody who is not in your stream marks a fourth point, and you say what that one shows that your three did not. That is the whole activity. It goes badly when people choose their three points to make a tidy story, so choose them before you have a story.

Nobody has ever regretted picking the ugliest offset. Several people have regretted picking the neatest.
