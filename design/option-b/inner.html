01 / Common bus — shared lecture

MON 11:00 · 60 MIN · ALL CH

Drift is the slow, directional error that accumulates while nothing appears to be going wrong. It is not noise: noise is symmetrical and averages out, and drift does not. In the first half of the lecture we separate the two by example, using three sets of readings taken across a semester by students who did not know that one of their instruments had been recalibrated halfway through.

The second half is about correction, which is where most of the interesting failures live. Correcting drift means deciding, in advance, what your reference is. If the reference is itself drifting, you get a method that is internally consistent and externally wrong — the most expensive kind of mistake, because every internal check passes.

Key claim

A method that cannot detect its own drift is not a method. It is a habit with documentation.

We close by writing the week’s reference statement together: one sentence naming what you are holding fixed, and one naming what you expect to move. You will need both for the return, and you will be asked in week eleven whether they held.

What you should be able to do afterwards

Name the reference in someone else’s method within two minutes of reading it. Say whether their correction procedure would catch a drift in that reference. Where it would not, propose the cheapest check that would — cheapest in their terms, not yours. Cheapness is part of the answer; a correct procedure nobody will run is a wrong procedure.

02 / Channels — parallel sessions

3 CH · SAME BRIEF · DIFFERENT EVIDENCE

Each channel takes the same reference statement into its own material. Read only your own channel to prepare, and read the other two before the week eleven cross-examination — the objections you will face come from the columns you skipped.

Channel A

Drift in the asking

Stream A · Field and interview · Tue 14:00

Your interview schedule drifts because you get better at the interview. By the eighth conversation you are asking a sharper question than you asked in the first, which means your early transcripts answer a question you no longer hold. This session we treat that as data rather than contamination.

Bring transcript one and transcript seven. We read the opening ninety seconds of each aloud and mark, live, every place the framing has moved. Then you write the reference statement you were actually working from in each — not the one on your plan.

Channel B

Drift in the instrument

Stream B · Build and instrument · Wed 10:00

Your build drifts because you keep fixing it. Each fix is locally correct and the accumulation is not, which is why the log is the assessed artefact and the build is not. We spend the first half reconstructing a timeline from three students’ logs and finding the exact commit where the measurement stopped meaning what the earlier readings meant.

Second half is a tolerance exercise: you state the largest error you would accept without re-running, in the unit your instrument actually reports, and defend the number. Round figures get challenged hardest.

Channel C

Drift in the record

Stream C · Archive and reading · Thu 09:00

Archives drift because the categories do. A file series that meant one thing in its first decade means something adjacent in its third, and nobody announces the change. This session works from a single finding aid that was revised four times, and asks what a researcher using only the current version would fail to find.

You will leave with one source note and one negative result: a claim the archive cannot support, stated precisely enough that someone could disprove you by producing the document.

04 / Activity — drift audit

SEQUENCE · 55 MIN · IN SESSION

Run in pairs during your channel session. The steps are ordered because each one consumes the previous one’s output; do not reorder them to save time.

  1. State the reference

    Write one sentence naming what your method holds fixed. No hedging clauses. Your partner reads it back and tells you what they think it means.

    5 min

  2. Find the earliest artefact

    Pull the oldest item in your folio that was produced under that reference. Do not tidy it first.

    5 min

  3. Mark the movement

    Working on your partner’s material, not your own, mark every point where the working reference appears to have shifted. Mark generously; false positives cost nothing here.

    15 min

  4. Rank by consequence

    Swap back. Of the marks on your own material, order them by how much they change the conclusion. Most will change nothing, and saying so out loud is the point.

    10 min

  5. Design the cheapest check

    Take the top-ranked drift and design a check that would have caught it within one week, costing under thirty minutes to run.

    15 min

  6. Commit the check

    Write the check into your plan with a date. This is the line the marker will look for in your return.

    5 min
